A pilot-scale activated sludge wastewater treatment plant (WWTP) operated with nitriﬁcation and
pre-denitriﬁcation was monitored with a set of on-line sensors for over 3 years. Wet-chemistry
ex-situ analyzers, UV and UV-Visible in-situ sensors and in-situ sensors based on ion-selective
electrodes (ISE) were used. New ISE sensors for ammonium, nitrate and nitrite, adapted to water
and wastewater matrices, have been released in recent years, With adequate quality control they
proved to be highly accurate and reliable in WWTP inﬂuents and activated sludge (AS) reactors even
at the end of the biological treatment zone, working at low ammonium concentrations
(1–2 mgN/l). The ammonium measurement was used to test several feed-forward and feed-back
aeration control strategies. The ﬁrst aim was to keep inorganic nitrogen compounds, i.e.
ammonium, nitrate and particularly nitrite, as low as possible in the efﬂuent, and within Swiss
national standards (,2.0 mgNH
4-N/l, ,0.3 mgNO2-N/l, 24 h average). All the strategies were
successful at keeping ammonium low and subsequently at gaining denitriﬁcation capacity to
signiﬁcantly reduce the total nitrogen discharge. Some control strategies however generated
temporary peaks of ammonium or even accumulation of nitrite.
Key words | activated sludge, aeration control, ammonium, in-situ sensors, ion selective electrode
INTRODUCTION
In the past three decades instrumentation, control and
automation (ICA) for wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs)
made major progresses in particular with regard to actuators,
computing power and data storage or control theory (see
Lynggaard-Jensen 1999; Olsson 2006 for reviews). However,
despite these advances, the search of appropriate instrumenta-
tion for real time control (RTC) at WWTPs remains a very
actual issue. As one of the weak links in ICA, sensors, and in
particular in-situ sensors for nutrients, have an increasing
potential (Jeppsson et al. 2002; Bonastre et al. 2005).
In this work, a difference is made between two
measurement methods:
† In-situ sensors measure directly in the process matrix,
without external pumping and without any sample
preparation. Together with a short response time, this
allows total data acquisition times of few seconds. Time
and costs for operation and maintenance (O þ M) can
also be signiﬁcantly lower than for ex-situ analyzers
(Winkler et al. 2004). In-situ sensors however necessitate
regular external calibration and quality control.
† Ex-situ analyzers require pumping (often in a closed loop)
and normally pre-treatment (ﬁltration) of samples. Succes-
sive samples are processed in a batch mode or Continu-
ously. Response times depend on analytical methods and
typically range from 5– 25 min for the whole system
(sampling, analyzer and pre-treatment). Ex-situ analyzers
often require time consuming maintenance and costly
reagents and regular exchange of spare parts.
In-situ sensors for temperature, pH, conductivity, redox
potential or suspended solids (SS) for example are common

practice and nearly every plant in Europe is equipped with
in-situ sensors for dissolved oxygen (DO) (see Vanrolle-
ghem & Lee 2003 for a review). This already enabled
improved monitoring and control in WWTPs, e.g. RTC of
aeration (DO setpoint) by varying air ﬂow or pressure or
RTC of sludge inventory by measuring SS in the AS reactor
and varying the waste sludge ﬂow.
The on-line measurements of nutrients with ex-situ
analyzers can be used for process control; to optimize
nitriﬁcation, denitriﬁcation or enhanced biological phos-
phorus removal (EBPR) by adjusting aeration intensity
(varying DO setpoints or intermittent aeration) or changing
internal recirculation or return AS ﬂows ( Ingildsen &
Olsson 2002; Jeppsson et al. 2002; Olsson et al. 2005 for an
overview). Ingildsen & Wendelboe (2003) for example
reported successful implementations of RTC for nutrient
removal in AS systems using miniaturized analyzers which
were directly submerged into the liquid medium. However
as mentioned earlier, the use of ex-situ analyzers can still be
associated with high costs (investment, reagents, mainten-
ance) and need for intensive skilled maintenance.
Recent developments of in-situ sensors for nutrients, in
particular ion-selective electrodes (ISE) and UV-Visible
sensors further facilitate the monitoring and control of
WWTPs (Rieger et al. 2002; Van den Broeke et al. 2006). In
this study ISE ammonium sensors were used for advanced
aeration control and ISE, UV-Vis and UV probes to
monitor nitrate and nitrite. ISE offer continuous measure-
ments with a very short response time ( Thomann et al.
2002), as well as high accuracy, less maintenance and high
tolerance to harsh conditions. If a proper quality control
concept for the in-situ sensors is in place, this offers new
and very interesting perspectives for process control.
Aeration makes up for the biggest part of the energy costs
of a WWTP and optimization strategies can improve the
overall performance of a plant signiﬁcantly. In addition,
controlling ammonia to a level below the efﬂuent limits
allows increasing the denitriﬁcation capacity and sub-
sequently reducing the overall nitrogen removal. A full scale
simulation study for a large WWTP, Zu ¨ rich-Werdho ¨ lzli in
Switzerland ( Siegrist et al. 2004) showed that aeration
strategies such as the feed-back or feed-forward control of
DO setpoints based on ammonium ISE signals could lead to
consequent costs savings combined with increased nitrogen
removal. The impact of ISE based control was estimated to
improve total nitrogen removal by 10% and to save up to 30%
energy costs. When considering an additional tax based on
nitrogen efﬂuent load the energy savings raised up to 35%.

relevance of ISE based control. A rapid estimate of costs and
savings can be made considering the following assumptions

ISE are based on a potentiometric principle, i.e. the
measurement of an electric potential difference between a
reference electrode, showing a constant potential, and a
measurement electrode. The measurement electrode is
made of a selective membrane, binding speciﬁc ions
reversibly, which inﬂuence its potential E. The difference
between reference and measurement electrodes depends on
the natural logarithm of the ion activity.
ISE for ammonium measures only NH
4-N and not
NH3-N, therefore the measurement electrode has to be
coupled to a pH and temperature sensor to calculate total
ammonia. Ions with binding properties toward the selective
membrane similar to the measurement ion may create
perturbations.
Table 2 gives indications on main disturbance ions for
ammonium and nitrate ISEs. A cross-sensitivity of 1:100
indicates that a concentration of 100 mg/l of the disturb-
ance ion induces a potential difference equal to 1 mg/l of the
measurement ion ( Winkler et al. 2004). Cross-sensitivity
depends ﬁrst on the total concentration of the disturbance
ion, corrected by an offset, and then on the variations of that
concentration, which requires an additional membrane for
constant compensation.
Two conﬁgurations were used in the pilot plant. In the
primary clariﬁer the sensor was equipped with an ammonium
ISE, and a pH-temperature probe for NH
4-NH3 compen-
sation. In the AS reactors, which have lower pH variations,
sensors were made of ammonium and nitrate ISEs. For these
sensors, constant pH compensation was made based on
average pH values. With reason to already low variations of
the potassium concentrations in the primary clariﬁer, the
sensors were not equipped with an additional potassium
electrode for correction but simply corrected with a constant
offset. The sensors come in the form of dip-in probes, made of
a polymer shaft (Ø 70 £ 1,200 mm) and a signal transformer
box at its top. It requires power and pressurized air for self-
cleaning (nozzles).
Three alternative calibration methods are possible
(Rieger et al. 2002; Thomann et al. 2002) A single-point
calibration using an underlying default calibration curve. A
two-point calibration, whereupon a linear curve is calcu-
lated or the implemented calibration curve is ﬁtted. Or
ﬁnally a ﬁve-point calibration curve by means of a standard
addition. For the presented work, a multiple-point cali-
bration curve was used as a ﬁrst calibration using ﬁltered
matrix from reactors as the medium for standard addition.
The sensors were later regularly recalibrated with one point
using the weekly control measurements from the laboratory.
Further standard addition experiments were carried out to
illustrate the quality of the measurements. Figure 2 shows
results for the ammonia ISE sensors in reactor Bio1
(measuring range 0 – 15 mg N/l) and Bio3 (0 – 1 mg N/l)
and nitrate in Bio1 (0 – 10 mg N/l). The sensors were very
stable along with the time, in particular those in use in
activated sludge reactors, as an example Figure 3 illustrates
the stability of the ammonia ISE in reactor Bio1 over a
period of 275 days.
The approximate maintenance time for the three pilot
plant ISE’s, including daily control, quick cleaning, weekly
lab reference measurements, monthly cleaning, membrane
and electrolyte replacement and calibration is 5 – 6 hours of
qualiﬁed staff per week i.e. less than 2 hours per week and
sensor. The main problem encountered in the routine
operation is the accumulation of dirt around electrodes.

Despite automated cleaning nozzles, and especially in pre-
treatment tanks (e.g. primary clarifier), the formation of a 
chemical films on membranes was often a problem. On the 
long term, the aging of the membrane is also a major issue as 
well. Membranes are changed every 3 - 4 months depending

The quality control of all devices in the plant follows a 
monitoring concept with a 2-step control chart approach 
developed at Eawag (Figure 3) based on the analysis of 
comparative measurements between sensors and a reference 
method. It combines statistical analysis, regression analysis, 
and graphic control charts with decision support rules. The 
method allows quick detection of abnormalities such as 
shifts, drifts, outliers and wrong calibration. Details can be 
found in Rieger et aL (2004b) and Thomann et aL (2002).

One remarkable effect can be observed in the switch
from control strategy 2b to 2c ( Figure 6 ). Control of phase
2c was almost the same as 2b except that aeration was kept
constant at 2 mgO

polishing step. In fact, due to the constant aeration of
reactor Bio3 alone ammonium was most of the time kept
under the upper control limit and as a matter of fact Bio2
was not aerated for prolonged periods. A clear nitrite
accumulation resulted, which could be triggered by several
factors. First of all it is likely that despite the constant
aeration in Bio3, the total aerobic hydraulic residence time
was not sufﬁcient anymore to oxidize all of the nitrite
produced, which therefore started slowly accumulating over
days. While in this case no temperature shock occurred that
could explain a change in the activity of AOB and NOB
another reason could be the modiﬁcation of oxygen mass
transfer into the activated sludge ﬂoc. Manser et al. (2005)
described its consequences on nitriﬁers kinetics comparing
a membrane pilot plant with the Eawag pilot plant
presented in this study. Depending on the NOB population
composition (high proportion of Nitrobacter genus) and
ﬂoc structure (large size and low SVI) and although it was
not clearly demonstrated, it is not to exclude that the longer
periods with aeration cut off inhibited NOB sufﬁciently to
induce their progressive wash-out and to trigger the
subsequent accumulation of nitrite.
CONCLUSION
The experience collected at the Eawag pilot plant with the
use of in-situ sensors for monitoring and control were
successful. ISE sensors proved to be reliable and accurate in
activated sludge reactors, even with working ranges as low as
1–2m g NH
4-N/l. Aeration strategies based on NH4 control are
conﬁrmed to be efﬁcient and a promising optimization tool
for WWTP. Recent ISE sensors are very costs competitive as
compared to traditional chemical analyzers and they consti-
tute therefore a serious option in WWTP’s. A set of basic
parameters was proposed for the approximation of a WWTP’s
critical size at which investment and maintenance costs for
ISE sensors would be recovered by savings on aeration energy.
Based on our experience and for a typical WWTP design the
critical size would be around 15,000 P.E., however this ﬁgure
need to be adapted in case of different speciﬁc constraints.
Different intermittent aeration strategies based on ISE
signals allowed an increase of the denitriﬁcation capacity
while keeping the ammonium below the Swiss legal threshold
of 2 mgNH
4-N/l (24 h ﬂow proportional). These strategies also
showed to have an impact on nitrite concentrations in the
efﬂuent and some periods clearly overpassed the Swiss
guidance value of 0.3 mgNO
2-N/l. It was shown that under
certain conditions intermittent control could possibly lead to
the inhibition of NOB and to a possible accumulation of nitrite
in the efﬂuent. Such side effects should be kept in mind while
designing control loops.
