1. Title of the Invention 
System and Method for Gas-Hazard Classification and Mitigation Using Fan Coast-Down Aerodynamic 
Densitometry with Thermodynamic -Residual Compensation and Chemoresistive Disambiguation  
2. Field / Area of Invention 
This invention belongs to the fields of embedded instrumentation, physical gas sensing and 
environmental safety control. It describes an apparatus and a method in which a small axial fan, normally 
used only as an actuator, is operated as a measuring element. The fan is spun up and its supply is 
interrupted. The time taken for each revolution during the ensuing free coast -down is recorded, and the 
resulting speed -decay record is fitted to a rotor -dynamics model that separates the speed -independent 
and viscous friction losses of the bearing from the aerodynamic loss of the blades. The aerodynamic 
coefficient is proportional to the density of the surrounding gas and is converted into a density estimate. 
That estimate is compared with the density that dry and humid air would have at the prevailing 
temperature, relative humidity and pressure, measured by a co -located environmental sensor. The 
remaining difference, the thermodynamic density residual, isolates the contribution of a foreign gas of 
molecular mass different from that of air. A metal -oxide chemoresistive (MQ -series) sensor supplies a 
second, physically independent signal. The pair of signals is used to assign the atmosphere to one of five 
hazard classes and to select a mitigation action appropriate to that class, including suppression of fan 
energisation and choice of a high or low vent path when a combustible gas is indicated.  
Low-cost gas monitoring in rooms, cabinets, cylinder stores and small workshops relies almost entirely 
on metal-oxide chemoresistive sensors. Such sensors respond to reducing and combustible gases and to 
organic vapours, and they are strongly affected by temperature a nd humidity, but they give little or no 
response to inert asphyxiants such as carbon dioxide at percent-level concentration or helium. They also 
cannot say whether a detected gas is lighter or heavier than air, which determines where it accumulates 
and therefore where it must be vented. Two consequences follow. An asphyxiant leak can progress 
without any response from the installed detector. A combustible leak may be answered by energising an 
exhaust fan, which is itself a potential ignition source, and by venting from the wrong height. A physical 
measurement of gas density, compensated for the ordinary variation of air density with the weather, 
supplies exactly the two pieces of information the chemoresistive sensor lacks. It indicates that the 
composition of the air has changed, and it gives the sign of the change, which is heavier or lighter than 
air. The present invention obtains that measurement from a fan that the protection system requires in 
any case. 
2.1 Technical Problem Addressed by the Invention  
● Metal-oxide chemoresistive gas sensors are not responsive to inert asphyxiants such as carbon 
dioxide and helium at the percent -level concentrations relevant to oxygen displacement, so a sensor 
of this kind alone cannot detect that class of hazard.

● A chemoresistive response does not indicate whether the gas responsible is lighter or heavier than 
air, so it cannot by itself choose between a high and a low ventilation path. Nor can it separate a 
combustible gas from a benign organic vapour that produces a com parable resistance change. 
● The density of ambient air changes by several per cent with ordinary variation of temperature, 
humidity and barometric pressure. This is larger than the density change produced by a percent-level 
gas admixture, so any uncompensated density measurement is dominated by the weather rather than 
by composition. 
● The drag experienced by a rotating fan depends on bearing friction as well as on gas density. Bearing 
friction varies from unit to unit and increases with wear. A fan-based measurement that uses only an 
overall quantity, such as steady speed at fixed drive or total coast -down time, therefore confuses 
mechanical wear with a change of gas composition.  
● Conventional automatic mitigation energises an exhaust fan whenever a gas thresho ld is crossed, 
irrespective of whether the gas is flammable. It also does not distinguish buoyant from dense gases 
when selecting the vent path.  
2.2 Definitions 
Coast-down. The free deceleration of the fan rotor after its electrical drive is removed, durin g which 
the only torques acting are bearing friction and aerodynamic drag.  
Revolution period (T k). The time taken by the rotor to complete revolution k during coast -down, 
obtained from the tachometer edges belonging to that revolution.  
Loss coefficients (a0, a1, a2). The three coefficients of the rotor deceleration model of Equation 3. a 0 is 
the speed-independent (Coulomb) friction term, a 1 the term proportional to speed (viscous bearing loss) 
and a2 the term proportional to the square of speed (aerodynamic  loss). a2 is proportional to the density 
of the surrounding gas. 
Aerodynamic density estimate (ρ̂). The gas density inferred from the aerodynamic coefficient using 
the commissioning calibration constant K (Equation 6).  
Thermodynamic reference density (ρ ref). The density that humid air would have at the temperature, 
relative humidity and pressure measured by the environmental sensor, computed from the moist -air 
equation of state (Equation 8). 
Thermodynamic density residual (r). The percentage by which the aerodynamic estimate departs from 
the thermodynamic reference (Equation 9). In clean air r is close to zero whatever the weather. A gas 
heavier than air drives it positive, and a gas lighter than air drives it negative.  
Standardised residual (z).  The density residual divided by its clean -air standard deviation established 
at commissioning (Equation 10).  
Chemoresistive response (s). The normalised response of the MQ-series sensor, defined as the negative 
natural logarithm of the ratio of sensing resistance to its clean-air reference (Equation 13).  
Friction lock. The practice of fixing the viscous coefficient a 1 at its commissioning value when fitting 
coast-downs for density estimation, so that only a 0 and a2 are free. This reduces the covariance between 
viscous and aerodynamic terms in the fit.  
Recommissioning. The automatic procedure invoked when the friction coefficients depart from their 
commissioning values. The density channel is withheld from classification, a set of clean -air coast -
downs is acquired, and the friction lock and calibration constant are re -established.

decides whether a chemoresistive response is caused by a buoyant gas, a dense gas or a vapour of 
negligible density effect.

4. Summary and Background of the Invention 
4.1 Practical Background and Limitations of E xisting Solutions 
A typical low -cost gas-safety unit consists of a metal -oxide sensor, a microcontroller, a buzzer and a 
relay that switches an exhaust fan. This arrangement answers one question: whether the resistance of the 
sensing film has fallen below a threshold. It performs acceptably for the reducing gases the film is 
designed for, such as liquefied petroleum gas, methane or alcohol vapour. It is blind in three situations 
that matter in practice. The first is an inert asphyxiant. A carbon dioxide rel ease from a dry-ice store or 
fire-suppression system, or a helium or nitrogen release from a cylinder, displaces oxygen without 
changing the film resistance. The second is the distinction between a combustible gas and a harmless 
solvent or cleaning vapour, both of which lower the resistance, so that the unit is either prone to nuisance 
alarms or insensitive. The third is buoyancy. Methane rises and accumulates at the ceiling, propane and 
butane sink and accumulate at floor level, and a unit that knows only the resistance cannot direct 
ventilation to where the gas is. 
The installed mitigation compounds the problem. Switching on an exhaust fan whenever the threshold 
is crossed is appropriate for a toxic but non -flammable gas. For a combustible gas it introduce s an 
energised electrical device into a potentially flammable mixture, which hazardous-area practice seeks to 
avoid. The information needed to act correctly is the molecular weight of the contaminant relative to air, 
which gives heavier or lighter, and whe ther it is chemically reducing, which gives combustible or not. 
The first is a physical property that a chemoresistor does not measure.  
Gas density does carry that information. At fixed temperature and pressure, admixture of a gas of molar 
mass M g into air  of molar mass M air changes density in proportion to the admixture fraction and to 
(Mg/Mair − 1). The difficulty is that the ordinary day -to-day variation of air density, caused by 
temperature, humidity and barometric pressure, is several per cent, which exceeds the effect of a percent-
level leak. Dedicated density meters, including vibrating-element transducers, spinning-rotor gauges and

4.2 Distinctions Over the Closest Reported Systems  
The building blocks of this invention are individually known, and the disclosure does not assert 
otherwise. The inference of gas density from rotor deceleration, with correction for non -gas drag, is the 
operating principle of the spinning rotor gauge (Fedchak et al., 2015). Species-dependent rotor damping 
has been reported by Blakemore et al. (2020). The decomposition of a coast -down into constant, linear 
and quadratic loss terms is standard in vehicle road -load testing (SAE J2263; 40 CFR 1066 Subpart D). 
The separation of friction and windage by retardation is standard in machine testing (IEEE Std 112). 
The use of fan spin -down to assess bearing friction is disclosed in US5621159A, and fan -load 
monitoring for failure prediction in US20060176186A1. The infer ence of air density from a fan 
operating point, and its calculation from temperature and pressure, are both disclosed in US9119979B2. 
Identification of a fuel -gas family from a change of blower speed is disclosed in EP2706300. 
Temperature and humidity compensation of semiconductor gas sensors is disclosed in US4730479A and 
US10520481B2, and automatic fan actuation on gas detection in CN102645908A. The distinctions 
therefore cannot rest on any of those operations. Table 3 maps the feature set against the clo sest 
references, and five differences are relied upon.  
First, the measurand is the ambient gas and not the machine.  In the road-load, machine-testing and 
fan-diagnostic art the coast -down characterises the rotating object, with air density treated as a kno wn 
correction or as a nuisance. Here the rotor is the known quantity, characterised once at commissioning, 
and the aerodynamic coefficient is used as a transducer output for gas density.  
Second, friction is separated and then locked.  US5621159A and the coa st-down-time literature use 
an aggregate spin-down quantity that is dominated by bearing condition. The present method fits the full 
three-term model to per -revolution timing. It then refits with the viscous coefficient fixed at its 
commissioning value, so  that bearing variation is absorbed by a 0 and does not leak into a 2. Figure 13 
and Table 13 show that an aggregate coast -time estimate is displaced by up to about 12 % under added 
bearing friction, whereas the separated estimate remains within about 0.65 % . 
Third, a residual is formed against a thermodynamic reference.  US9119979B2 derives air density 
either from a fan operating point or from pressure and temperature, and uses whichever it obtains for 
flow control. It does not compute both and difference them. In the present invention the thermodynamic 
reference, including humidity, is subtracted from the aerodynamic estimate, so that ordinary weather -
driven density change cancels and only composition -driven change remains. The sign of the residual 
gives the buoyancy of the contaminant.  
Fourth, the density residual and the chemoresistive response are fused as two orthogonal axes.  
EP2706300 identifies a fuel family from blower speed alone, with a known gas deliberately injected. 
Chemical-array approaches (Verga ra et al. , 2012) rely on trained classifiers over many chemical 
channels. Here one physical axis, which responds to molar mass whatever the chemistry, and one 
chemical axis, which responds to reducing species whatever the mass, are combined by an explicit two-
axis rule. The pair separates five classes that neither axis separates alone. These are clean air, inert 
asphyxiant, buoyant combustible, dense combustible and low -density-effect vapour. 
Fifth, mitigation is selected by class and by buoyancy.  CN102645908A energises a fan whenever a 
gas is detected. Here the fan is driven to full ventilation for an asphyxiant, is left unenergised for a 
combustible, and the vent path is chosen high for a buoyant combustible and low for a dense one. An

unresolved high chem oresistive response is escalated to the conservative combustible action. The 
density-channel self-check (friction monitoring and recommissioning) prevents a worn fan from being 
read as a gas. 
4.3 Inventive Combination and Cooperative Arrangement  
The contribution is a cooperative arrangement in which each element conditions the next, not any single 
operation within it. The per-revolution timing makes the model fit possible. The model fit makes friction 
separation possible. Friction separation makes the aerod ynamic coefficient a density transducer rather 
than a wear indicator. The thermodynamic reference turns that density into a composition residual. The 
residual supplies the physical axis on which the chemoresistive response is disambiguated, and the 
resulting class selects a mitigation that depends on flammability and buoyancy. The friction coefficients 
produced by the same fit feed back into a self -check that withholds the density axis and recommissions 
the fan when its mechanics change. Removing any one el ement either restores the confusion it was 
introduced to resolve or removes a class from the decision. This is shown by the ablation of Section 8.8, 
where the omission of thermodynamic compensation, of friction separation, or of the density channel 
altogether each lowers macro-F1 from 0.759 to between 0.366 and 0.667.

4.6 Industrial and Practical Applicability  
Laboratories and teaching facilities that store compressed gases, dry ice or cryogens a re a primary 
application, because inert asphyxiants are present alongside flammable gases and solvent vapours, and 
because a single low-cost unit is preferable to separate oxygen, flammable -gas and toxic-gas detectors. 
Gas-cylinder stores and cabinets bene fit from the buoyancy -dependent vent selection. Domestic and 
commercial kitchens using liquefied petroleum gas or piped natural gas benefit from the distinction 
between dense and buoyant fuel gases and between fuel gas and cooking vapour. Cold rooms and 
beverage-dispensing areas using carbon dioxide, battery rooms, small workshops and equipment 
enclosures that already contain a cooling fan are further applications. In the last case the existing fan can 
be given a sensing role by adding revolution timing and  the processing of Section 7. The unit is 
constructed from mass -produced components (a microcontroller, a brushless fan with tachometer, a 
combined temperature, humidity and pressure sensor, and a metal -oxide sensor), so the invention is 
capable of industrial manufacture. 
5. Objective(s) of the Invention 
The primary objective is to provide a single low -cost embedded unit that measures the density of the 
surrounding gas by means of a fan that the unit also uses for ventilation. The unit compensates that 
measurement for weather and for mechanical wear, combines it with a chemoresistive measurement to 
identify the class of gas hazard, and selects a mitigation action appropriate to the flammability and 
buoyancy of the gas. The specific objectives follow.  
● To acquire the period of every revolution of a fan during free coast -down after its supply is 
interrupted, and to derive rotational speed and angular velocity from those periods.  
● To fit the coast -down record to a rotor -dynamics model that separates speed -independent friction, 
viscous friction and aerodynamic loss, and to extract an aerodynamic coefficient proportional to gas 
density. 
● To stabilise the density estimate against bearing variation by fixing the viscous coefficient at its 
commissioning value, and by averaging a plurality of coast -downs. 
©VIT IPR & TTCELL 
10 
● To compute a thermodynamic reference density from the measured temperature, relative humidity 
and pressure, and to form a signed, standardised density residual that is insensitive to weather and 
sensitive to composition. 
● To normalise the chemoresistive response against an environment -adjusted clean-air reference, and 
to combine it with the density residual in an explicit two -axis classifier that distinguishes clean air, 
inert asphyxiant, buoyant combustible, dense comb ustible and low-density-effect vapour. 
● To select a mitigation action by class, including suppression of fan energisation for combustible 
gases, choice of a high or low vent path by buoyancy, and conservative escalation of unresolved 
high chemoresistive responses. 
● To detect a change of fan mechanics from the friction coefficients, to withhold the density channel 
while that change is unresolved, and to recommission the calibration automatically from clean -air 
coast-downs. 
● To support calibration at more than o ne fan starting speed, and to record raw and derived quantities 
so that each decision can be reconstructed afterwards.  
6. Working Principle of the Invention (in brief) 
The unit executes a measurement cycle in which the fan is used first as a sensor and then, if required, as 
an actuator. Figure 4 gives the complete sequence and Figure 1 the architecture.  
Environmental and chemical sensing. A combined temperature, relative-humidity and pressure sensor 
(BME280) and an MQ -series metal-oxide sensor are read cont inuously, typically once per second. The 
sensing resistance Rs of the MQ element is obtained from its load -resistor voltage. 
Coast-down acquisition. The fan is driven to its operating speed and allowed to stabilise, and then its 
drive is removed. Each revo lution is timed from the tachometer edges. The revolution periods give a 
record of angular velocity against time from about 2 900 RPM down to about 160 RPM in roughly 18 –
19 s for the fan characterised in Section 8. The first 0.3 s after cut -off is excluded  so that electrical 
transients of the drive do not enter the fit.  
Loss separation. Once the drive is removed, the rotor obeys I dω/dt = −(τ 0 + bω + kρω2). The constant 
term is dry bearing friction, the linear term viscous bearing loss and the quadratic ter m aerodynamic 
drag of the blades, which is proportional to gas density ρ. Figure 5(b) shows that the aerodynamic term 
dominates the deceleration at high speed and friction dominates near rest. The whole coast -down 
therefore carries separable information on  both. The coefficients are obtained by fitting the solution of 
this equation to the timing record.  
Density and residual. With the viscous coefficient locked at its commissioning value, the aerodynamic 
coefficient a2 is averaged over three coast -downs and divided by the commissioning constant K to give 
the density estimate ρ̂. The thermodynamic reference ρref is computed from temperature, humidity and 
pressure, and the residual r = 100(ρ̂/ρref − 1) and its standardised form z are formed. In clean air z stay s 
near zero. A dense gas drives it positive and a buoyant gas negative.  
Fusion, classification and mitigation.  The pair (z, s) is classified by an explicit rule (Table 8). A 
negligible chemoresistive response with a significant residual indicates an inert asphyxiant, so the fan is 
driven at full speed, the vent is opened and an alarm is raised. A significant chemo resistive response 
with a negative or positive residual indicates a buoyant or dense combustible respectively, so the fan is 
not energised, the high or low vent path is opened and an alarm is raised. A chemoresistive response 
©VIT IPR & TTCELL 
11 
with no significant residual i ndicates a vapour of small density effect, which leads to partial ventilation 
and a notification, unless the response is high enough to be escalated to the combustible action.  
Self-check. The friction coefficients from the same fit are compared with their commissioning values. 
A departure indicating wear withholds the density channel and triggers recommissioning from clean -air 
coast-downs, after which the viscous lock and the calibration constant are re -established.

6.2 Statement Regarding Hardware Implementation  
The invention comprises a gas -hazard detection and mitigation apparatus built from commercially 
available components: an axial fan with a tachometer output (101, 102), a supply-voltage monitor (103), 
a combined temperature, humidity and pressure sensor (104), a metal-oxide chemoresistive sensor (105), 
a microcontroller hosting the processing and decision layers (200, 300), a fan drive capable of switching 
and inhibiting the fan (401), a servo -operated vent (402), audible and visual indication (403), a local 
display (404) and a recording interface (501). What distinguishes it is the manner in which these 
components act on one another. The fan is both a transducer a nd an actuator. The friction state of the 
fan determines whether its transducer output is admitted to the decision. The decision determines not 
only whether but how the fan and vent are operated, including whether the fan may be energised at all. 
The output of the apparatus is a set of actuator commands that change the physical state of the protected 
space. 
6.3 Technical Contribution 
● A mass-produced fan is converted into a gas -density transducer by timing its coast -down, without 
adding a dedicated density sensor. 
● Bearing friction, which dominates aggregate coast -down time and varies with wear, is separated 
from aerodynamic loss by fitting a three -term model, and is kept out of the density estimate by 
locking the viscous term. 
©VIT IPR & TTCELL 
12 
● The variation of air density wit h weather, which is larger than the effect of a percent -level leak, is 
cancelled by differencing against a thermodynamic reference computed from co -located T, RH and 
P measurements. 
● A physical axis that responds to molar mass is paired with a chemical axis that responds to reducing 
species. This separates inert asphyxiants, which chemoresistors miss, from combustibles, and dense 
combustibles from buoyant ones. 
● The mitigation is selected so as not to energise the fan in a combustible atmosphere, and the vent  
path is chosen by the sign of the density residual.  
● The same fit that yields density also yields the friction coefficients, which provide a self -check and 
trigger automatic recommissioning of the density channel.

7.2 Sensing Layer (100) 
Optical tachometer (102). In the preferred embodiment an infrared reflective or slotted optical sensor 
views the fan blades, or a marked hub, and produces one pulse per blade passage. The Hall output of a

7.4 Coast-Down Model and Separation of Loss Mechanisms (202)  
After the drive is removed the only torques acting on the rotor are bearing friction and aerodynamic 
drag. Following the standard three -term representation of rotating -body run-down (Table 2, entries 3 –
5), the rotor obeys: 
Equation 3: Rotor Coast-Down Model (202)

 a0 = τ0/I,    a1 = b/I,    a2 = kρ/I (3) 
where I is the rotor moment of inertia, τ0 the speed-independent (Coulomb) bearing friction torque, 
b the viscous friction coefficient, k an aerodynamic constant of the blade geometry and ρ th e gas 
©VIT IPR & TTCELL 
17 
density. The normalised coefficients have units of rad/s2 (a0), s−1 (a1) and rad−1 (a2). Because I and 
k are fixed properties of the fan, a 2 is proportional to ρ, while a 0 and a1 describe the bearing. The 
three terms scale differently with ω (Figure 5(b)). The aerodynamic term dominates at the start of 
the coast-down and friction near its end, so a record covering the whole speed range identifies all 
three. 
Equation 4: Coast-Down Fit (202) 
 (a0, a1, a2, ω0) = arg min Σk [ ωk − ω̂(tk; a0, a1, a2, ω0) ]2,    over k with tk ≥ 0.3 s (4)

down of the fan characterised in Section 8 the fit gives, for example, a 0 = 1.5626 rad/s 2, a 1 = 
0.014370 s−1 and a2 = 1.4837 × 10 −3 rad−1, with RMSE 0.0773 rad/s and R 2 = 0.9999988. 
The free three -coefficient fit supplies a 0 and a1 to the friction monitor (302). For density estimation a 
second fit is made with a1 held at its commissioning value a 1,lock, leaving a0, a2 and ω0 free. The viscous 
and aerodynamic terms are partly collinear over the speed range of a coast -down. Locking a 1 prevents 
bearing scatter from being exchanged with a 2, and it transfers any change in friction to a 0, where it does 
not affect density.

 a1 ≡ a1,lock  ⇒  (a0, a2)j,    j = 1, …, N

7.6 Thermodynamic Reference and Density Residual (204, 301)  
Equation 8: Thermodynamic Moist -Air Reference Density (204) 
 ρref = (P − pv) / (Rd · TK) + pv / (Rv · TK) 
 pv = (RH / 100) · psat(T) (8) 
where P is the measured absolute pressure (Pa), T K the absolute temperature (K), RH the relative 
humidity (%), p v the partial pressure of water vapour, p sat(T) the saturation vapour pressure over 
water, Rd = 287.06 J kg −1 K−1 and Rv = 461.5 J kg −1 K−1 the specific gas constants of dry air and 
water vapour. A Magnus -type expression is used for p sat(T) The CIPM -2007 formulation (Table 
2, entry 8) may be substituted where higher accuracy is required. ρ ref is the density that the gas 
would have if it were clean humid air. 
Equation 9: Thermodynamic Density Residual (301)  
 r = 100 · ( ρ̂ / ρref − 1 )    [%] (9) 
The residual cancels the weather -driven variation of air density that is common to ρ̂ and ρref, and 
it retains the composition-driven part that only ρ̂ contains. 
Equation 10: Standardised Residual (301)  
 z = r / σr (10) 
where σr is the clean-air residual standard deviation at commissioning (0.1476 % for three coast -
downs). z expresses the residual in units of the clean -air scatter of the installed fa n and is the 
physical axis of the classifier. 
Figure 8 illustrates the effect of the residual in clean air: when the aerodynamic estimate is compared 
with a fixed reference density, the weather alone produces apparent anomalies of up to about 4 %, 
whereas the thermodynamic residual remains inside the clean -air band.

Equation 11: Physical Basis of the Residual Sign  
 r ≈ x · ( Mg / Mair − 1 )    [%, with x in % vol]  (11) 
For a dilute admixture of gas of molar mass M g at volume fraction x into air of molar mass M air at 
the same temperature and pressure, the relative density change follows from the ideal -gas law.

whose density effect is negligible. The sign of r therefore indicates whether the contaminant is

Equation 12: MQ Sensing Resistance (205)  
 Rs = RL · ( Vc / Vout − 1 ) (12)

7.9 Buoyancy-Aware Mitigation and Escalation (304, 400)  
The mitigation selector (304) maps each class to the actions of Table 8, and Figure 12 shows the logic. 
The distinguishing features are that the fan is not energised when a combustible gas is indicated, which 
removes the fan motor as an ignition source during the hazard, and that the vent path is selected by the 
sign of the density residual. A buoyant gas is released through a high vent and a dense gas through a low 
vent. An inert asphyxiant, which cannot ignite, receive s full forced ventilation. A strong chemoresistive 
response that the density channel cannot attribute to a buoyant or dense gas is not dismissed as a nuisance 
vapour. It is escalated to the combustible action, so that uncertainty in the density axis resolv es towards 
the safer outcome. The high and low vent paths may be realised by servo -actuated dampers at different 
heights or by one servo selecting between ducts.

● A coast -down is accepted only if the supply -voltage record confirms that the drive was off

● An unresolved strong chemore sistive response is escalated to the combustible action rather than

7.15 Equivalent Implementations  
Any fan or blower with a timeable rotor can serve as the sensing element, including centrifugal blowers 
and the cooling fans of existing equipmen t. The tachometer may be a Hall output, an optical interrupter 
or reflective sensor, or back-EMF sensing on a brushless motor with the drive disconnected. The model 
may be fitted by nonlinear least squares on the ODE solution, as described, or by regression of the finite-
difference deceleration on (1, ω, ω 2). The latter is simpler but less accurate. The thermodynamic 
reference may use any moist -air formulation, and CO 2 background can be included as in CIPM -2007. 
The chemoresistive axis may use another MQ -series element, several elements, or an electrochemical 
cell, and s may be defined against any stable reference. The classifier thresholds may be replaced by 
regions of the (z, s) plane learned at commissioning without changing the arrangement.  
7.16 Complete Operating Sequence  
Figure 4 gives the operating sequence. S1: initialise and load the calibration constants K, a 1,lock, a0,ref, 
σa0, σa1, R0 and σr. S2–S3: read T, RH, P and Rs. S4: spin up the fan and allow it to stabilise. S5: remove 
the drive. S6–S7: time each revolution and compute RPM and ω. S8: discard revolutions within 0.3 s of 
cut-off. S9: fit the free three-term model for a0 and a1, and refit with a1 locked for a2. S10: repeat S4–S9 
three times and average a 2. S11: compute ρ̂. S12: compute ρref, r and z. S13: check friction, and if wear 
is indicated withhold density and recommission. S14: classify (z, s). S15: apply the mitigation, record 
the cycle and repeat.

8.10 Decision Latency 
The experimentally measured decision latency of the proposed three-coast-down method had a median 
of 77.23 s, with a range of 55.7–80.1 s. 
The latency consists primarily of the three coast -down durations together with a constant processing 
and control interval of approximately 21.3 s.  
For the single-coast-down configuration, the median decision latency was 25.95 s, corresponding to 
the coast-down duration plus approximately 7.3 s.  
The chemoresistive channel responded substantially faster, with a median response time of 
approximately 4 s. 
Consequently, for reducing gases the apparatus can generate a provisional chemoresistive indication 
within seconds, while the density -dependent classification becomes available following completion of 
the coast-down sequence. 
The measured latency is therefore do minated by the physical coast-down dynamics rather than by 
computational processing. 
Alternative implementations using a lower -inertia fan, shorter fitting window or fewer coast -down 
cycles could reduce the latency, although the sensitivity -performance trade-off observed in Table 15 
would need to be considered.

8.12 Physical Experimental Validation and Limitations  
The experimental evaluation in Sections 8.1 –8.11 was performed using the physical hardware 
implementation described in Section 7 and the breadboard prototype shown in Figure 2. The 256 test 
cases therefore constitute a physical experimental dataset acquired from the implemented apparatus, 
rather than a purely simulated or synthetic evaluation.  
The campaign included physical measurements from the fan tachometer, fan supply -voltage 
monitoring, environmental sensor and chemoresistive sensing channel. The recorded measurements 
comprised 155,563 revolution periods and 84,736 environmental and chemore sistive samples. The 
©VIT IPR & TTCELL 
36 
processing and classification procedures described in Section 7 were applied to these recorded 
measurements. 
The experimental campaign establishes physical operation of the disclosed method under the 
conditions specified in Table 10, i ncluding variation in gas species and concentration, temperature, 
relative humidity, pressure, fan operating speed and bearing friction.  
The following limitations remain.  
 The performance statistics reported in this section include commissioning cases as we ll as test 
cases. They therefore should not be interpreted as independent held -out test-set performance.  
 The experimental conclusions are limited to the gas species, concentration ranges, 
environmental conditions, operating speeds and friction conditions represented in Table 10. 
Performance outside these ranges has not been established by the present campaign.  
 The experimentally observed density resolution, approximately 0.45% vol for helium and 
0.67% vol for carbon dioxide at the three -sigma criterion under the principal clean-test 
conditions, does not establish suitability for all occupational exposure or regulatory detection 
thresholds.  
 Heavy-combustible recall at the classification level was 0.333. Detection of dense 
combustibles at low concentration therefore relies partly on the escalation mechanism, which 
introduces nuisance escalation for some vapour conditions.  
 The aerodynamic model does not explicitly account for all possible dependence of the 
aerodynamic constant on gas viscosity and Reynolds n umber beyond the implemented speed -
specific calibration.  
 Thermal drift of bearing friction within an individual coast -down and airflow disturbances in 
the surrounding environment may affect the density measurement.  
 The sensing spin-up energises the fan. In a suspected flammable atmosphere, this presents an 
ignition-safety consideration. In the described embodiment, measurement spin -ups are 
suspended when a combustible class has already been declared, while an ignition -protected fan 
represents an alternative implementation.  
 The measured decision latency is primarily determined by the physical coast -down dynamics 
and the number of coast-down measurements used. Alternative hardware configurations may 
provide different latency characteristics.  
Despite these limitations, the physical experimental campaign provides direct experimental evidence 
that the disclosed apparatus and processing chain can acquire fan coast -down, environmental and 
chemoresistive measurements and use the resulting signals for density esti mation, environmental

under the tested conditions.  
9. Discussion 
9.1 Achievement of the Stated Objectives  
Each objective of Section 5 is supported by a result of the evaluation in Section 8. The coast -down is 
timed per revolution and fitted to a three -term model with a median R 2 of 0.9999987, and the 
aerodynamic coefficient yields density within ±0.0029 kg/m 3 of reference. Friction locking and 
averaging reduce t he clean-air scatter to 0.13 –0.15 %. The thermodynamic residual holds the clean -air 
anomaly inside ±3σ across the tested weather ranges. The two -axis classifier separates five classes with 
macro-F1 0.759 against 0.366 for the chemoresistive sensor alone. M itigation inhibits the fan for every 
combustible record. The friction monitor detects both wear events and restores the density channel after 
recommissioning. Speed-specific calibration keeps the clean -air residual within 0.03 % at three speeds.  
9.2 Inventive Step of the Claimed Combination  
The objection to be met is that every operation in this invention is individually known, and Section 3 
concedes this. Gas density from rotor deceleration with non -gas drag subtracted is the spinning rotor 
gauge. The thre e-term coast -down is the road -load equation. Friction and windage separation by 
retardation is in IEEE Std 112. Fan spin -down for bearing friction is in US5621159A. Air density from 
a fan operating point, or from pressure and temperature, is in US9119979B2 . Gas family from blower 
speed is in EP2706300. Environmental compensation of metal -oxide sensors is in US4730479A and 
US10520481B2, and fan actuation on gas detection is in CN102645908A. The case for inventive step 
rests on the cooperative arrangement, an d three arguments are advanced.  
The first concerns the role reversal of the fan and the residual that makes it work. In every identified 
coast-down reference the rotating body is the object of measurement and the gas is a known correction. 
To use a mass-produced ventilation fan as a gas transducer, three obstacles must be overcome together. 
The friction of a cheap bearing dominates the coast -down (Figure 6). The weather moves air density by 
more than a leak does. And friction drifts with wear. The three -term fit with viscous lock addresses the 
first. The thermodynamic residual addresses the second, and it is not suggested by US9119979B2, which 
treats the two density sources as alternatives. The friction self-check addresses the third. The ablation of 
Table 15 shows that omitting any one of them removes most of the benefit.  
The second concerns the pairing of axes. The prior physical-property analysers (Table 2, entries 1, 2 and 
17) measure a known gas or binary mixture. EP2706300 identifies a fuel deliberately  injected. None 
pairs a density residual with a chemoresistive response. That pairing is not a generic fusion of more 
sensors. It is the specific combination in which each axis resolves the ambiguity of the other: mass 
without chemistry, and chemistry with out mass. It is what allows inert asphyxiants to be detected and 
buoyant combustibles to be separated from dense ones.  
The third concerns the output. The decision selects whether the fan may be energised at all, and at which 
height the space is vented. The se are consequences of the density sign and the chemoresistive response 
together. No identified reference produces a flammability - and buoyancy -dependent actuation, and 
CN102645908A, the closest mitigation reference, does the opposite by energising the fan  for every 
detection.

9.3 Statutory Character Under Sections 3(k) and 3(f)  
Section 3(k) of the Indian Patents Act, 1970 excludes mathematical methods, algorithms and computer 
programmes per se . The relevant question under current examination practice for c omputer-related 
inventions is whether the invention, considered as a whole, solves a technical problem by technical 
means with a technical effect. The technical problem is that low-cost gas-safety units cannot detect inert 
asphyxiants or determine gas buoy ancy and flammability, and that a fan's coast -down is corrupted by 
weather and wear. The technical means are physical: a rotating fan whose drive is interrupted, a 
tachometer, environmental and chemoresistive transducers, and a fan drive, servo vent and al arm. The 
technical effect is a physical measurement of gas density and the resulting change in the physical state 
of the protected space, including withholding fan energisation and choosing the vent path. Claims should 
accordingly be framed as an apparatus and a method of operating it in which the fan is de -energised, its 
coast-down is timed and processed, and actuators are commanded. The equations belong in the claims 
as the means by which the sensed state is converted into an actuation decision, not as th e subject of the 
claims. 
Section 3(f) excludes the mere arrangement or re -arrangement of known devices each functioning 
independently in a known way. The parts here do not function independently. The fan is alternately 
transducer and actuator. The friction state of the fan determines whether its transducer output is admitted. 
The environmental sensor determines the reference against which the fan output is interpreted. The 
density sign determines how the chemoresistive response is read, and the resulting cl ass determines 
whether the fan may be energised. That mutual conditioning is what is put forward as the invention.  
9.4 Deployment and Residual Limitations  
The unit requires no trained classifier, no gas -specific calibration of the density channel and no 
additional density sensor. The calibration constants are obtained by an explicit clean -air commissioning 
procedure that the unit can repeat by itself.  The main residual limitations are those of Section 8.12: the 
decision latency is of the order of a minute f or three coast-downs, the density resolution does not reach 
occupational exposure levels, and the behaviour of the sensing spin -up in a flammable atmosphere is to 
be specified.
